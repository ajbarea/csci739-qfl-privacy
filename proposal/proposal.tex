\section*{Motivation}

In federated learning, clients train a shared model by sending gradients instead of their
data~\cite{mcmahan2017}. On classical neural networks that protects little: given the model and
one client's gradient, an attacker can adjust a fake input until its gradient matches, and
recover the real input~\cite{zhu2019}.

Whether this attack works on variational quantum circuits (VQCs) is open, and the papers
disagree:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Kumar et al.~\cite{kumar2023}: expressive encodings stop the attack, because an attacker
        using a smaller model than the client's gets stuck in many false local minima.
  \item Papadopoulos et al.~\cite{papadopoulos2025}: the attack works, even on batches, once the
        model has enough parameters.
  \item Heredge et al.~\cite{heredge2025}: circuits that are easier to train also leak more about
        their inputs.
\end{itemize}
This project tests where each claim holds on small, simulated circuits.

\section*{Research questions}

Core, answered in the report:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*.}, left=2.5em]
  \item With the parameter count fixed, do more encoding layers~\cite{perezsalinas2020} protect
        the data better than more trainable layers? Four and eight qubits.
  \item Does shot noise in the client's gradient stop the attack?
\end{enumerate}
Extensions, in this order, as time allows:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*.}, left=2.5em, resume]
  \item How do batch size, an unknown label, and a trained model affect the attack?
  \item Does noise simulated from real IBM devices stop the attack?
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Method}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Model.} Each input feature is encoded as an $R_Y$ rotation, repeated $r$ times,
        each followed by $\ell$ trainable layers ($R_Y R_Z$ rotations and a CNOT chain). The
        output is the average $\braket{Z}$ over all qubits, so every feature affects the
        gradient. Four qubits, then eight.
  \item \textbf{Threat model.} The server follows the protocol but tries to learn from what it
        sees: it knows the circuit and its parameters and sees one client's gradient.
  \item \textbf{Attack.} Gradient matching~\cite{zhu2019}: search for the input whose gradient is
        closest to the observed one (L-BFGS-B with random restarts).
  \item \textbf{Outcomes.} Each attack ends \emph{recovered} (right input, compared mod $2\pi$),
        \emph{ambiguous} (different input, same gradient), or \emph{stuck} (local minimum).
        I report rates over at least 20 seeds per setting. Parameter counts include only
        parameters with a nonzero gradient: the last layer's $R_Z$ gates never affect the output.
  \item \textbf{Noise.} The client's gradient is computed with noise; the attacker matches it
        with the noiseless model. Shot noise for RQ2; Qiskit Aer noise models from IBM Heron
        calibration data (\texttt{ibm\_kingston}, \texttt{ibm\_fez}, \texttt{ibm\_marrakesh}) for
        RQ4.
  \item \textbf{Tools.} PennyLane for the attack (it needs gradients of gradients), Qiskit Aer for
        noise. Code: \url{https://github.com/ajbarea/csci739-qfl-privacy}.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Preliminary result}

First run: four qubits, one example, known label, untrained model, five seeds per row.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccc@{}}
  \toprule
  Encoding reps $\times$ trainable layers & Parameters & Recovered & Ambiguous & Stuck \\
  \midrule
  $1 \times 1$ & 4   & 2\rlap{$^*$} & 2 & 1 \\
  $1 \times 4$ & 28  & 5 & 0 & 0 \\
  $4 \times 1$ & 28  & 3 & 0 & 2 \\
  $4 \times 4$ & 124 & 4 & 0 & 1 \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}\\[2pt]
{\small $^*$One exact, one within 0.07 rad of the true input.}
\end{center}

At 28 parameters, more encoding layers stopped the attack 2 of 5 times; more trainable layers,
0 of 5. That points the same way as Kumar et al.~\cite{kumar2023}, though their attacker uses a
smaller model than ours, and five seeds is too few to conclude anything.

\section*{Deliverables}

A LaTeX report answering RQ1 and RQ2, centered on a map of attack success over encoding layers
and parameter count, plus any extensions that finish. A class presentation in weeks 12--14.
All circuits are simulated and at most eight qubits, so the results show where each claim holds
at that size, not how it scales.
